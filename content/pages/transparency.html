IRIS on this site

What IRIS may say here.

The <•> at the bottom of every page opens IRIS. On this site it follows written rules:

  1. It answers only from OMARO's public material: this website, published documents, released features and OMARO's policies.
  2. It stays off the founder's private life, OMARO's finances and investors, internal tools, unreleased plans and security details.
  3. When it can't answer, it says so and points to support@omaro.org. It never guesses.
  4. Every answer names the pages it came from.
  5. It gives no legal, financial or medical advice.

Your question goes to Anthropic's model to write the answer. OMARO keeps only the questions IRIS couldn't answer, as the text alone, with no address or other identifier, for 30 days, so the site can fill the gaps. Everything else is gone when the answer arrives.

In short: IRIS runs on your device and keeps what it learns there. Without an account, OMARO collects no personal data about you. We never sell your data, never share it for advertising, and never use it to train any AI model. Four things leave your device, and only when you press a button for them: the address of a code repository you ask IRIS to read, a website's address when you ask for its public code, your network address when you ask IRIS to check your connection from outside, and a problem report you choose to send. If you make an account, which is optional, it holds a number with no email, name or phone, and anything it syncs is locked on your device so OMARO can't read it.

Who we are

OMARO PBC is a public benefit corporation. Its public benefit is technology that returns authority to people. IRIS is its product: IRIS, the co-operating system for the internet. In this policy, "we" and "OMARO" mean OMARO PBC, and "you" means the person using IRIS or visiting our website.

What IRIS keeps on your device

  • In Firefox: your settings, a fingerprint of each policy you checked, with the sentence that set each answer (so IRIS can tell you when it changes and show what it said before), your history of readings, the names of companies each site's pages contacted, counts of what IRIS blocked for you, and your monthly summary. Firefox keeps them in the add-on's own storage.
  • On Linux: the apps on your computer, the sites and companies each one talked to, the readings of makers' policies, an inbox of what changed on your computer, the old value of any system setting you changed with Fix it (so you can put it back), and your settings. IRIS reads your system's and desktop's own settings on your computer to tell you which ones send data; it changes one only when you press Fix it. They are kept in an encrypted file in your home folder. The key to that file is kept in your system's keyring, which only your account can open.
  • On GrapheneOS (when it ships): the same kinds of things as on Linux, kept in the app's own encrypted storage on your phone.

None of this is sent to OMARO. We can't see it, and we can't hand it to anyone. You can delete all of it at any time with Forget everything in IRIS's settings, and removing IRIS deletes it too.

What IRIS reads from websites

When you turn on reading, IRIS fetches privacy policies and terms straight from the websites that publish them. On Linux and GrapheneOS it reads the policies of the companies that make your apps. These requests go from your device to that website and nowhere else. They carry no cookies, no sign-in and no referrer. The website sees an ordinary request from your network address and may log it under its own policy. OMARO never sees these requests.

What leaves your device, and only when you ask

  1. Read the code. When you press Read the code, IRIS sends the repository's address to OMARO's code server. The server reads that repository's public code, asks an AI model to describe what the code does, and sends the reading back. Nothing about you or your device is sent, and the model sees only public code.
  2. A company's public code. For a website that isn't on the list IRIS ships, pressing Look for its public code sends that website's address to OMARO's code server.
  3. Check from outside. When you press Check from outside, IRIS asks OMARO's code server which company owns the address the internet sees for you. The server sees your current network address to answer. It keeps no record of it.
  4. Your account, if you make one. An account is optional in the Firefox add-on and in IRIS Lite. It is a 16-digit number with no email, name or phone. Making one or signing in sends OMARO's account server your number, a name for the device (such as "Firefox on Linux"), a code made from your number and that device so a reinstall takes its old place, and a public key made from your 12 words. The 12 words never leave your device. With sync on, IRIS sends what it keeps and your look and comfort choices, each locked on your device with a key made from your 12 words, so OMARO can't read them.
  5. Report a problem. When you press Send to OMARO, IRIS sends the report you just read in full to OMARO's code server: the kind of problem, what you wrote and, only if you ticked the box, the version of IRIS, your system, your language and the view you were on. The code server locks each report on arrival with a key only OMARO holds, so the server itself can't read it, and keeps nothing about who sent it. OMARO's own review desk collects the locked reports, opens them, and deletes them from the code server. Security problems are never sent this way.

Your browser or your computer sends your network address with every request to any server, ours included. Our code server uses it for one thing: it counts how many new readings each network address started in the last hour, so that one network can't use up the day's readings for everyone. That count is deleted after one hour.

What our servers keep

  • Readings of public code, by repository and by commit, so the next person who asks gets the same reading at once. A reading describes public code. It holds nothing about who asked for it.
  • The hourly count described above, deleted after one hour.
  • Problem reports you send, locked until OMARO collects them, then kept by OMARO to fix IRIS. The code server also counts how many reports each network address sent in the last hour (five at most), in memory only; that count is gone within the hour.
  • Accounts, if you make one: a keyed fingerprint of your number (never the number itself), the public key made from your 12 words, your two-step key if you turn it on, each device's name, kind and dates, a fingerprint of each device's sign-in, your plan and its end date, the codes you redeemed, and your synced copies, which are locked and can't be read by OMARO. No IP addresses and no logs of who asked. Close my account deletes all of it.
  • We keep no browsing history, no analytics and no logs that name you.

The companies we rely on to run the server keep their own records under their own policies: Render hosts the code server and its database in the United States, Anthropic runs the AI model that reads public code, and Vercel hosts our website. We send them only what this policy describes. We have asked none of them to keep anything about you, and we will tell you here if that changes.

Our website

Our website sets no cookies and runs no analytics, advertising or tracking scripts. See our cookie policy.

What we never do

  • We never sell your data, and we never share it with advertisers, data brokers or anyone else.
  • We never use your data to train AI models, ours or anyone else's. Nothing you do in IRIS becomes training data.
  • We never show you advertising.
  • We never ask for an email, a name or a phone number, and the Firefox add-on never needs an account.

Your rights

You can see, export and delete everything IRIS keeps, from IRIS's own settings, without asking us. Outside an account, we hold no personal data about you, so a request to us under any privacy law will find nothing to delete; an account you can close yourself in IRIS, which deletes it from our server. Write to us anyway and we will answer in writing within a month. You can also complain to your data protection authority.

How long things are kept

Outside an account, we store no personal data about you. You can delete an account and its synced copies at any time with Close my account in IRIS.

  • On your device: until you delete them. On Linux, the record of which companies each app talked to covers the last 30 days, the record of each policy fetch covers the last 90 days, and the inbox keeps its last 1,000 entries from the last 400 days; older entries are deleted on their own.
  • On our servers: readings of public code are kept until the code changes or until we remove them. The hourly count is deleted automatically after one hour. A problem report waits, locked, on the code server until OMARO collects it (within the hour), and is then deleted there; OMARO keeps the report itself until the problem is fixed, and deletes it when you ask.

Where data is kept

What IRIS learns is kept only within your device, wherever you are. Our code server is in the United States and holds only readings of public code, the hourly count and locked problem reports waiting to be collected. OMARO's account server, once it opens, holds only what the account section above lists, locked where it says so.

Children

IRIS is for everyone and collects no data from anyone, including children under 13. We do not collect personal information from children.

Security

Code readings travel over HTTPS. On Linux, IRIS's own records are encrypted on your disk, and the network helper that blocks addresses runs with only the rights it needs and asks for an administrator's password before it changes anything. Our security posture is published and describes how IRIS is built and tested.

Changes to this policy

We will tell you about changes at least 30 days before they take effect, in IRIS itself and on our website. A change that would collect more about you will never apply to what IRIS kept before the change, and you can turn off the feature it concerns.

Disputes

If you have a problem with how we handle your data, write to us first. If we can't put it right, you can bring a claim in court where you live, under the laws that protect you there.
