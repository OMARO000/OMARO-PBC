OMARO PBC · Version 2.0 · November 2026 · Read first

Why IRIS exists

Every website and app you use comes with rules about you: what it collects, who it sells to, whether it trains AI on what you write, whether you can ever take it to court. Those rules are real and they bind you. They are also written for lawyers, spread across several long pages, and changed without much notice. Almost nobody reads them, and the people who do rarely understand what they agreed to.

OMARO's principle is short: clarity returns agency. You can only decide for yourself what you understand. IRIS reads the fine print and shows you, in plain words, what it means for you, with the exact sentence behind every answer.

IRIS is a co-operating system: it works alongside the browser and phone you already use, as a layer that makes the internet's rules readable. It runs as a Firefox add-on today, with a Linux app and an Android app (GrapheneOS first) to follow.

Commitments that hold across every part

These apply to every layer this series describes, now and later.

  1. Reading happens on your device. IRIS fetches the policy pages of the site you are on and reads them on your computer or phone. Nothing about what you visit is sent to OMARO.
  2. Every answer shows its sentence. A tile that says "At risk" links to the sentence in the policy that decided it, and to the page it came from. You can check every answer yourself.
  3. IRIS says when it couldn't read. If a page sits behind a login, is built by scripts IRIS could not run, is in a language IRIS does not read yet, or is blocked by a bot check, IRIS says so, page by page. It never shows a result it did not earn.
  4. Precision comes before coverage. A wrong "At risk" frightens people for nothing; a wrong "Respected" reassures them falsely. Both count as release-blocking errors. When the wording is unclear, IRIS says the policy doesn't say, rather than guessing.
  5. Accuracy is measured on sites IRIS has never seen, and published. The method and the current numbers are in Part 1.
  6. IRIS reports what a policy says. It gives no legal advice and makes no finding that a company follows or breaks any law. What a company does can differ from what its policy says; IRIS reads the policy.
  7. No advertising, no data sales, no tracking of the people who use IRIS. OMARO is funded by people who choose to pay for it.

Plain language, in three layers

Every technical idea IRIS shows has three layers:

Layer What it is Example
1. Technical term The accurate name, kept for anyone who wants it Binding arbitration clause
2. Plain name What IRIS shows first Going to court: Arbitration only
3. Plain meaning What it means for you, in a sentence You give up your right to take a dispute to court; a private arbitrator decides instead.

The plain name leads. The technical term is available. The plain meaning is one tap away. The test for a plain meaning: someone who has never thought about the idea understands it on first read and knows what it means for them.

Other languages

IRIS reads English policies today. Reading other languages will happen on your device, as reading does now. Plain names are authored for each language by people who think in it; they are never machine-translated, because a phrase that is correct but carries no meaning to its reader is just a different kind of jargon.

The series

IRIS works in layers, each named for what it shows you: Your rights and Your agreement read policies and terms, What this page runs reads what a page loads and does, Read the code reads a public code repository, Company record keeps the documented enforcement record, the HAI Standard measures how a company's AI behaves, and Infrastructure shows who hosts and delivers the service. The HAI Standard is the one layer with its own name, because it is a standard organizations are measured against.

The preamble and all seven parts make one document, the IRIS Methodology, published as one page and one PDF. Each part joins it when the layer it describes ships, so the document only ever describes what IRIS does today.

IRIS Methodology, Part 1: Your rights, policy reading

OMARO PBC · Version 2.0 · November 2026 · Applies to the Firefox add-on from version 0.5.4

This part describes how IRIS finds a site's privacy policy and terms, how it reads them, how it decides each answer, and how its accuracy is measured. It is written so that anyone can check what IRIS does.

1. What IRIS reads

When you open IRIS on a site, it reads the site's policy set:

  • The privacy policy. IRIS looks for it among the page's links (link text such as "Privacy Policy" and paths such as /privacy). If you are already on a policy page, it reads that page. If the page it reaches is a short hub that only links to the full policy (common for large companies), it follows that link once. When a legal page holds every document as tabs (a privacy tab, a terms tab, rewards terms and so on), IRIS reads the tab named for the privacy policy as the policy and the tab named for the terms as the terms, and leaves the program and promotion terms out.
  • The terms of service. Court access and AI training are often written there. IRIS looks on the page you are on, then on the privacy policy.
  • Related policy pages, up to 8 (or 25 with "Read deeper" or the Deep setting): regional supplements, cookie policies, children's policies and similar pages linked from the policy and the terms.

IRIS reads pages on the same site, or on the same company's other domain (for example redditinc.com for reddit.com). It skips:

  • another company's terms (for example an embedded "Google Terms apply" notice);
  • pages written for someone else: businesses, developers, advertisers, drivers, job applicants, donors, employees;
  • archived or dated earlier versions of a policy, translated copies, and promotion rules ("Terms apply");
  • blog, help and tag pages that talk about privacy without being a policy.

How pages are fetched

  • Pages are fetched by your own browser, without your cookies, so IRIS reads the same public policy anyone would see.
  • PDFs are read with the open PDF.js reader bundled inside IRIS.
  • Pages built entirely by scripts can be read in a hidden background tab if you turn on full reading (Firefox asks your permission once).
  • IRIS identifies itself honestly: it uses your normal browser and never disguises its requests or rotates fake identifiers.

When IRIS can't read a page

IRIS lists every linked page it could not read and why: it couldn't be opened, sits behind a login, is built with scripts, is in another language, or was stopped by a bot check. If IRIS reached the privacy policy through a link and could read fewer than 35 sentences of it, it says so and shows no result. When you are on the policy page itself, it shows the result with a note that the policy is unusually short.

2. The seven answers

IRIS answers seven questions about your rights. Each answer has one of four states:

Respected The policy protects you on this point
Limited There is a condition, an exception or an opt-out you have to use
At risk The policy works against you on this point
Policy doesn't say IRIS found no clear statement

Training AI on your data Not used, or only with your opt-in Used unless you opt out Used, with no way to opt out
Selling your data Not sold Shared with partners for advertising, or a denial hedged around the legal meaning Sold, or the policy says its sharing counts as a sale
Deleting your data You can delete it, and the only exceptions are narrow ones tied to a specific reason (the law, fraud or safety, money owed, disputes, backups) You can, but the company may keep data for open-ended reasons ("business purposes", no reason given), or copies of your data remain You cannot delete it

How an answer is decided

IRIS splits every page into sentences and matches each one against written rules for each question. The rules are public in the IRIS source code. A few principles decide most cases:

  • Only the company's own practice counts. Rules for users ("you may not use the content to train a model", "you may not sell your account") never count as what the company does.
  • Notices about the law are never admissions. A list of your rights ("you may opt out of the sale of your data") or a hypothetical ("were we ever to sell...") never shows the company sells. A plain admission ("we sell or share your information as follows") does.
  • A promise must cover your data. A promise about one item ("we will not sell your phone number", "we do not use email content to train models") never shows the whole tile as respected.
  • Exceptions win over the headline. "We do not sell your data other than for targeted advertising" is sharing for advertising. "You can delete your data, but we may not be able to delete some of it" is a limited right, unless every exception names a narrow reason: "we keep what the law requires, or what we need to collect fees you owe" leaves the right Respected. Nearly every policy names these exceptions, and the law allows them; IRIS shows them as evidence so you can judge them yourself.
  • The general rule wins over a narrow one. "IP addresses are deleted after 100 days" never outweighs "we keep information as long as necessary".
  • The main terms decide court access. Arbitration written only in side terms for paid features or bookings shows "Courts, but arbitration for some services".
  • Headings, questions and examples about other companies state nothing.

The overall result

Result When
Your rights at risk AI training, selling, deletion or court access is at risk, or any two answers are at risk
Review recommended Anything is limited, at risk or not stated, short of the above

A right the policy says nothing about keeps a site from "Your rights protected". Silence is either a choice or an oversight, and the standard asks that every right be met in writing. | Your rights respected | All seven answers are respected |

Hidden tiles (Settings → Tiles to show) still count toward the overall result.

Evidence

Open any tile to see what the question means, what this policy does, up to three sentences that decided it, and the page each came from. "Copy findings" puts the whole reading on your clipboard; "Save for review" saves the reading and the full text of every page read to your own computer. Nothing is sent anywhere.

3. Policy changes

With policy change alerts on (Settings), IRIS remembers on your device a fingerprint (a SHA-256 hash) of each privacy policy you check and what each answer was. When the policy changes, it tells you which answers changed. "Forget everything IRIS remembers" clears this.

4. Pre-read readings

OMARO will publish readings of popular sites prepared ahead of time, signed so that IRIS can prove they came from OMARO and were not altered. IRIS uses a pre-read only when it cannot read the live policy itself, labels it with the date it was read, and tells you when the live policy differs from the version OMARO read. A dataset that fails its signature check is never used.

5. Accuracy

How it is measured

  • Confirmed set. Every site used to write or fix a rule is kept, with its full saved text and the answer for each question. Every new version of IRIS must read all of them the same way, or the difference is reviewed. The set has 98 sites.
  • Held-out runs. A batch of sites IRIS has never been tuned on is read once, before any rule is changed, and scored against the full text. That first score is the published number. Only then are the errors fixed and the sites added to the confirmed set.
  • The release gate. A version may be released to testers only with zero wrong "At risk" and zero wrong "Respected" on a fresh held-out run. Misses ("doesn't say" where the policy says) are tracked separately.

Reviews to date were done with AI assistance and are being confirmed by OMARO.

Results so far

Run Date Reader Sites Answers right Wrong "At risk" Wrong "Respected" Sites with either

Run 3 was read in three batches of fresh sites. Each batch was scored cold, with the reader as it stood before that batch, and its errors were fixed before the next batch. 252 answers were scored. Fourteen answers on two sites are left out, because IRIS read the wrong page as the privacy policy there; that fault is counted separately. OMARO confirmed the nine answers where the policy text left room for more than one reading before this score was published.

IRIS has not yet passed its release gate. Every run found wording the rules had not met before; every error was fixed and added as a test, and the next run uses fresh sites. These numbers will be updated after every run.

What comes next

Written rules miss wording they have not seen. OMARO is preparing a small reading model, trained on sentences OMARO has confirmed, from an open base model with a public license. It will run on your device, beside the rules, and replace them only when it scores better on fresh sites. Its base model, training sources and scores will be published.

6. Limits

  • IRIS reads English policies today.
  • IRIS reads what a policy says, which can differ from what a company does.
  • Pages that need a login, or that block automated reading, are reported as unread.
  • Policies change; a reading reflects the text on the day it was read.
  • IRIS gives no legal advice and makes no finding that a company follows or breaks any law.

7. Corrections

If IRIS misreads a site, use Copy findings or Save for review and send it to governance@omaro.org. Every confirmed misread becomes a test that every later version must pass.
